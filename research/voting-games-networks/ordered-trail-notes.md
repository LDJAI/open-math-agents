# Ordered lightpath routing

**Target.** Given a finite undirected $k$-edge-connected graph and distinct terminals $v_0,\ldots,v_k$, must there exist a trail visiting them in the prescribed order? Vertices can repeat on the trail. Each physical edge can be used once. Equivalently, route the $k$ consecutive demands using pairwise edge-disjoint paths, then concatenate. Narula-Tam, Lin and Modiano studied the question for wavelength assignment in reconfigurable optical networks in 2002. Timothy Chow's August 2026 status update lists even $k$, $k=3$ and $k=5$ as known and odd $k\ge 7$ as apparently open. Left To Solve lists the question as awaiting a Lean statement. [Listing](https://lefttosolve.com/p/mo-26890) · [Problem and status update](https://mathoverflow.net/questions/26890/is-every-k-edge-connected-graph-also-k-trail-ordered) · [2002 paper](https://www.mit.edu/~modiano/papers/J18.pdf)

## A checked routing theorem for rings

Take a ring with any number $n\ge3$ of vertices and any nonnegative integer link capacities. A capacity is the number of parallel physical channels on a link. The two smallest capacities sum to the edge connectivity of the ring. If that sum is at least $k$, **every ordered chain of $k$ demands can be routed**, with repeated terminals allowed. The Lean theorem `OrderedCycle.route_ordered_ring` chooses between the two actual cyclic arcs for each demand and proves that load is at most capacity simultaneously at every link. Distinct copies of each link can then be assigned to its users independently.

1. Omit consecutive equal terminals, leaving $m\le k$ nonempty demands. Route every demand clockwise. At each internal terminal one incoming demand and one outgoing demand cancel. Exactly, the load at link $e$ is the number of hops wrapping past label zero plus $1_{v_0\le e}-1_{v_m\le e}$. Hence any two loads differ by at most one.
2. Let $e$ have minimum capacity $c$. Choose the global direction giving smaller load $a$ there. Thus $2a\le m$. If the two directions tie, take the one with smaller maximum load. When $2a=m$, one orientation has no load greater than $a$, because the range is at most one and reversing all arcs replaces every load $x$ by $m-x$.
3. Every load now lies below or at both $a+1$ and $m-a$. If $c>a$, routing is already feasible. Otherwise flip any $a-c$ demands using $e$ to their complementary arcs. This leaves exactly $c$ on $e$ and at most $(m-a)+(a-c)=m-c$ on every other link. Its capacity is at least $k-c\ge m-c$.

The handwritten proof uses $m$ nonempty hops. The Lean theorem permits consecutive equal terminals directly by choosing the empty arc in either direction. `ordered_ring_routes.py` implements the construction and independently walks each returned route, checking its endpoints, used links, and every capacity. Its reproducible enumeration covers 149,183 instances, plus one separate consecutive-repeat test. The finite enumeration is an implementation check. The theorem's quantifiers range over all ring sizes and demand counts.

**Prior work.** Frank's integral edge-routing criterion, restated as Corollary 2.4 of Frank, Shepherd, Tandon and Végh (2001), already implies this ring existence result. Briefly, a prohibited configuration would require two crossing tight cuts. Pair capacities are at least $k$ and each demand crosses any cut at most once. Both cuts could be tight only if all $k$ demands cross both, and all four of their bounding link capacities are $k/2$ since every pair of these capacities sums to at least $k$. For odd $k$ this is impossible. For even $k$, the terminal chain alternates between diagonally opposite regions and returns to its starting region. In each of the four regions the demand boundary is even and its two bounding links sum to $k$, so no odd region exists. The deliverable is the short constructive proof and its checked formalization. [Corollary 2.4](https://egres.elte.hu/tr/egres-01-09.pdf)

## Gluing and lifting the construction

In a network whose underlying simple graph is a cactus, each block is a cycle or a bridge. The block-cut tree specifies which blocks a given terminal-to-terminal path must traverse. In a fixed block project every outside vertex to the articulation vertex through which it reaches the block. These projections turn the original list into another ordered list of the same length, possibly with repeats. On a bridge the capacity is at least $k$. In a cycle any pair of distinct links disconnects a component even when the rest of the cactus is present, so the ring theorem applies. Join the local routes in block-cut-tree order for each hop, using no route in a block where the two projections coincide. Blocks have disjoint edge sets and every link respects its capacity. This gives an edge-disjoint routing for all $k$ simultaneously across this family.

A related sufficient condition applies to blocks with additional links. Suppose a closed walk goes through all needed terminals and articulation vertices of a block, uses physically distinct link bundles at all its steps, and its two smallest bundle capacities sum to at least $k$. The walk may revisit a vertex. Fix one occurrence on the walk for each needed vertex, and route their occurrence indices as a ring. Projecting the routes back can identify vertices but cannot identify distinct physical bundles, so preserves the capacity bounds. Extra visits to vertices are permitted by the conjecture. This statement and the block gluing above are handwritten corollaries of the checked ring theorem.

For a nonvacuous simple-graph realization at arbitrarily large $k$, replace each vertex of a positive-capacity cactus by a clique of size at least the maximum of $3k$ and the number of incident physical edges. Attach channel endpoints at distinct ports in each clique. A cut splitting a clique crosses at least $3k-1\ge k$ edges, and all other cuts inherit the cactus guarantee. Route the sequence of containing cliques first. Choose each inter-clique path simple, so each of the $k$ original hops needs at most one port-to-port or endpoint-to-port connection in a given clique. At most $2k$ clique vertices are endpoints of those connections. Route each nontrivial connection through a distinct unused auxiliary vertex. The resulting two-edge paths are pairwise edge-disjoint. This family includes arbitrarily large simple graphs and arbitrary terminal choices therein.

## The obstruction in a general network

For odd $k$, the cuts on which all $k$ consecutive demands saturate a size-$k$ edge cut form a chain when oriented toward $v_0$. Indeed, the terminals must alternate across each such cut, so $v_0$ belongs to every chosen side and $v_k$ belongs to none. Crossing global minimum cuts force an even cut size. To see this, denote the four nonempty intersection regions in cyclic order by $A,B,C,D$. Sum the capacities of the two crossing cuts and compare with the boundaries of $A,C$, then $B,D$. Each comparison forces the missing diagonal ($BD$ or $AC$) to have zero capacity. All four region boundaries consequently equal $k$. Writing the adjacent inter-region capacities as $x,y,z,w$ gives $x=z, y=w$. The two original cuts have capacities $2x,2y$. For odd $k$ they cannot exist. Since the chosen sides all contain one endpoint and omit the other, the no-crossing property implies nesting. In `OddCuts.lean`, `odd_min_cuts_cannot_cross` verifies the no-crossing argument for arbitrary finite edge lists with parallel edges allowed.

**General compression theorem.** For odd $k$, a counterexample with the fewest vertices among finite undirected multigraphs has at most one unordered minimum cut crossed by all $k$ demands.

Suppose there are two. Orient both shores to contain $v_0$, and use nesting to write them as $X \subsetneq Y$. Put $Z=V \setminus Y$. The layer $Y \setminus X$ is nonempty and contains no terminal because both cuts separate every even-indexed terminal from every odd-indexed one. Contract $X$ to $s$ and $Z$ to $t$, discard loops, and preserve multiplicities. Every $s$–$t$ cut has at least $k$ edges, while both endpoint degrees are exactly $k$. Edge-Menger gives $k$ disjoint $s$–$t$ paths exhausting both boundaries. Lifting their two endpoint edges yields $k$ edge-disjoint paths from $X$ to $Z$ whose internal vertices belong to the terminal-free layer. A direct edge from $X$ to $Z$ is one of these paths by itself.

Keep the induced subgraphs on $X$ and $Z$. Replace each of the selected paths by a distinct edge joining its actual end vertices. All other layer edges may be discarded. This preserves the boundary edge incidences on each shore. The smaller multigraph $H$ remains $k$-edge-connected:

- A cut separating the two intact shores has exactly $k$ crossing edges.
- If just one shore splits, choose the side of the cut lying entirely within that shore. Its crossing edge count is the same as in $G$.
- If a cut splits both shores, write $d$ for the number of crossing edges internal to $X$. Adding the sizes of the two original cuts induced by the two nonempty pieces of $X$ gives $2d+k \ge 2k$. Thus $d \ge \lceil k/2\rceil$. The same bound holds independently within $Z$. Their internal edges alone already contribute at least $k$.

The Lean theorem `ShoreCompression.rematch_preserves_connectivity` verifies this cut-preservation argument for physical edge lists and arbitrary new pairings of the boundary incidences. The existence of the disjoint paths and the lifting step use the handwritten edge-Menger argument.

Every original terminal remains in $H$. Any ordered routing in $H$ lifts to $G$ by expanding the new edges to their fixed disjoint paths. The expansions are edge-disjoint from the retained shore edges, and revisiting an internal nonterminal vertex is allowed. Thus $H$ would be a smaller counterexample, contradicting vertex minimality.

**Shore with at most one requested terminal.** This second compression works for either parity of $k$. Let $X$ be a shore of a size-$k$ cut containing at most one requested terminal. Replace $X$ by a single vertex and preserve every boundary edge. Any original trail projects by dropping the internal edges. Conversely, take the sole terminal as root $r$ of $X$, or any vertex of $X$ if there is no terminal. Contract the complement of $X$ to $o$. Every $o$–$r$ cut has at least $k$ edges, while the degree of $o$ equals $k$. The $k$ edge-disjoint paths from $o$ to $r$ furnished by Menger exhaust all boundary edges. Removing each first boundary edge leaves an arm from its port to $r$, possibly of length zero. All arms are mutually edge-disjoint. Expand every visit to the contracted vertex by joining the incoming and outgoing port arms via $r$. An initial or final visit needs only one arm. A trail uses each port at most once, so the expansions preserve edge-disjointness and the order of all requested visits. Contracting a shore preserves $k$-connectivity. Therefore every shore of a minimum-size-$k$ cut that has more than one vertex must contain **at least two distinct required terminals** in a vertex-minimal counterexample.

The universal simple-graph and multigraph questions are equivalent. If a multigraph is a counterexample, replace each vertex by a clique of size at least $k+1$ and its number of incident edges, and attach the inter-clique edges at distinct ports. The new simple graph remains $k$-edge-connected. Choose one representative terminal in each requested clique. Every trail between those representatives projects to a trail in the original multigraph by dropping the internal edges. Thus a putative proof for all simple graphs would also cover all multigraphs. The minimality assertion above specifically uses the multigraph class so that compression can retain parallel links.

A second attempted proof reserves the first leg and applies the even case to the remaining legs. In a $k$-regular graph with nonadjacent first terminals, every candidate path has an internal vertex. Removing the path reduces that vertex’s degree to $k-2$, so the remaining graph cannot be $(k-1)$-edge-connected. This invalidates the inductive use of the even case.

The compression eliminates multiple saturated cuts from a minimal counterexample. With one saturated cut, the terminal-to-port assignments from opposite shores still need to match. With no saturated cut the argument supplies no routing. No proof of the general listed conjecture or its negation results.
