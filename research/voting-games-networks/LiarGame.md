# Even splitting in the Rényi–Ulam game

Dömötör Pálvölgyi asked on May 3, 2014 whether, when every class of surviving candidates has even size, splitting each class in half can ever require more yes/no questions in the worst case than another first question. The respondent has a fixed total lie allowance. A candidate belongs to class r when it can still tolerate r incorrect answers. Arbitrary subsets are legal questions. This models identification with adversarially corrupted answers. A finite counterexample would settle the entire universal question.

## A reduction for every lie bound and horizon

Write B(h,r) for the sum of binomial(h,j) over j from 0 through r. In a fixed strategy lasting exactly h questions, a candidate with remaining budget r is compatible with exactly B(h,r) different answer transcripts. An error-position set determines the transcript, since the previous answers determine the next question. The transcript and candidate conversely recover the error positions. A winning strategy needs pairwise disjoint transcript sets. A strategy that stops early can be extended to full depth.

**Unused-leaf extension.** Fix any winning strategy on the candidates with positive remaining budget, at a horizon of h. Each unused h-bit transcript can accommodate one extra zero-budget candidate. To insert one, answer each possible question according to its assigned transcript at that depth. The old strategy still determines the questions' membership on all old candidates; its membership on the new candidates is free to choose. The new candidates cannot lie, so each is compatible with exactly its assigned transcript. Consequently if the positive-budget candidates alone are feasible, the maximum number of zero-budget candidates that can accompany them is exactly

    2^h − sum over positive-budget candidates c of B(h, remaining budget of c).

This proof works for adaptive strategies and gives a constructive extension.

**Every counterexample survives deletion of all original zero-budget candidates.** Suppose a strategy wins in h questions and the even first split fails at h. The two even-split children have identical budget histograms. Pascal's identity says their volumes at h−1 sum to the parent's volume at h, hence each is at most 2^(h−1). By the extension lemma, a failing child of this volume is already infeasible on its positive-budget candidates alone. Deleting the original zero-budget candidates changes none of those positive-budget survivors, and any originally winning strategy still wins after the deletion. Thus a comparison of even splits with arbitrary splits can focus on positions with no original zero-budget candidates.

**Exact optimality with any single occupied positive budget.** Suppose there are 2u candidates with budget r≥1 and 2v with budget zero. In any winning first split, one answer branch retains at least u of the 2u candidates at budget r; all of the rest of these 2u candidates have budget at least r−1. Drop the original zero-budget candidates and lower enough budgets to get exactly u at r and u at r−1. This easier state is winnable within the remaining horizon. An even-split child adds v zero-budget candidates to that state. Its sphere volume fits by conservation, so the extension lemma accommodates them. This proves even splitting is optimal for these starting states for arbitrary u, v and r. No priority claim is made for these consequences.

The unresolved case has at least two distinct positive allowances. Comparisons where a proposed question selects at least half of each class (or at most half of each) cannot improve the even split either: in one branch each cumulative count at or above any remaining allowance is at least the corresponding balanced count, making that branch at least as difficult. Crossings between classes remain the obstacle.

**At least six positive-budget candidates are necessary for a counterexample, for every lie bound.** With at most four, the only remaining possibility is two candidates apiece at two distinct positive budgets r>s. More generally, allow any even number in the higher class and exactly two in the lower class, with the zero-budget candidates removed as above. The only proposed question still to consider selects more than half from one class and fewer than half from the other. Choose the branch retaining more than half of the higher class at budget r; exactly one fewer than half of the lower class stays at s. From the balanced child, move one unit of budget from a candidate at s to one at r−1, and give any further excess higher-class candidates one extra unit each. Since r−1≥s, this first transfer spreads the two budgets. By the convexity lemma of Fraknói et al., it weakly increases the number of questions needed. The lemma applies with unrestricted question size and incurs no exceptional elimination since s≥1. Giving further candidates extra budget also weakly increases difficulty. This branch therefore takes at least as many questions as the balanced one. [Fraknói, Márton, Simon and Lenger, Lemma 3](https://real.mtak.hu/191883/1/2104.01664v4.pdf)

## An obstruction to a general midpoint proof

A natural proof would show that the coordinatewise midpoint of two solvable integer histograms is solvable whenever it has integer entries. This stronger statement is false. With five questions, four candidates of budget one can use the words 00000, 00111, 11001, 11110 (minimum distance three). Two candidates of budget two can use 00000 and 11111. Their midpoint consists of two candidates of budget one and one of budget two.

For any first question at the midpoint, consider the branch that retains the budget-two candidate. If this branch also retains k of the two budget-one candidates at full budget, its four-question volume is 11 + 5k + (2−k) = 13+4k. For k≥1 this exceeds the 16 available transcripts. For k=0 the opposite branch contains three budget-one candidates, who cannot be distinguished in four questions: after any question some branch retains at least two of them at budget one, using all eight three-bit transcripts, and even the third candidate still requires a transcript. Thus the midpoint is infeasible. A proof of the original claim must exploit constraints specific to the two children of the same even parent. In this example the branch with both budget-two candidates would also inherit two new zero-budget candidates, exceeding its capacity.

## Exact computations

`liar_game_search.cpp` computes the largest number of zero-budget candidates that can be accommodated, enumerating the choices of how many positive-budget candidates to ask about in each class. Each answer branch receives the selected truthful candidates at their old budgets, and its remaining candidates lose one allowance. A wrong answer for a candidate with budget one consumes one unit of that branch's zero-budget capacity. Eligible penny capacities of the two branches add. The program maximizes this sum over first questions and compares it to the largest even penny count for the even split. It uses the sphere bound, pairwise indistinguishability, and constructive binary codes for pruning. These computations use C++, independently of the Lean files.

```sh
c++ -O3 -std=c++17 liar_game_search.cpp -o liar_game_search
./liar_game_search 7 14 38
./liar_game_search 14 8 56
```

| Maximum lie budget | Maximum positive-budget candidates | Even nonempty positive-budget histograms | Horizons enumerated | Result |
| --- | --- | ---: | --- | --- |
| 7 | 14 | 3,431 | 1–38 | No counterexample |
| 14 | 8 | 3,059 | 1–56 | No counterexample |

Both regions cover **every horizon and all even zero-budget counts** when combined with an explicit code. For N candidates of positive budget at most r, take the binary simplex code on k = ceiling(log2 N) message bits. Each nonzero linear functional is one coordinate, giving length 2^k−1 and minimum distance 2^(k−1). Repeat all coordinates ceiling((2r+1)/2^(k−1)) times; this gives minimum distance at least 2r+1. The required lengths for the two table rows are 30 and 56. Thus at any total horizon at least 31 or 57, respectively, positive-budget candidates in either balanced child can be distinguished nonadaptively. Any additional zero-budget candidates fit by the extension lemma whenever the original game could be won at that horizon. The region with no positive-budget candidates is ordinary binary search. The two rows overlap and their counts must not be added as unique positions. An independent direct minimax implementation checked 2,688 state/horizon outcomes against the optimized recurrence across a smaller domain.

**Verification scope.** `LiarGame.lean` proves the volume identity and that the even split minimizes the worse child volume. `LiarGameExtension.lean`, `LiarGameMasks.lean`, and `LiarGameOccupancy.lean` formalize adaptive decoding, the transcript-to-error-pattern bijection, and the exact criterion for adding zero-budget candidates. `LiarGameHomogeneous.lean` proves the same-sign comparison. In `LiarGamePennies.lean`, `homogeneous_halving_with_pennies` verifies optimality for any one positive allowance and any even number of additional zero-budget candidates. The theorem `restore_initial_pennies` verifies that any counterexample with several positive classes survives removal of all original zero-budget candidates. The six-candidate lower bound uses the paper argument and the published convexity lemma. The C++ search supplies separate computational evidence. The unrestricted question remains open.
